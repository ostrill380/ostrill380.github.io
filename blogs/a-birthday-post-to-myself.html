Happy Birthday | A Birthday Post to Myself

Zhaoxi Mei4 min readEnglish translation

I've seen plenty of birthday posts on WeChat Moments. Open one and you usually find an introduction, a few classmates' impressions of the birthday person, and their good wishes: all the ingredients of a warm, harmonious, thoroughly happy class. Fortunately, I have never been part of this little performance, in which appearances bear so little relation to reality. I say fortunately because I really cannot bear to make classmates rack their brains for a few flattering words about me. It seems an unreasonable thing to ask. If aliens landed on Earth one day to investigate relationships among Chinese university students, and began with class WeChat accounts, they would probably reach entirely the wrong conclusions. Presumably they would never have studied anthropology, and would not know that fieldwork requires more than skimming the surface.

Birthdays mean different things to different people. As a child, I often heard the saying, “A child's birthday is the day their mother suffered.” I wonder whether it leaves some children feeling a little guilty about celebrating. I have always admired people who remember every friend's birthday; my admiration for those who carefully choose birthday gifts knows almost no bounds. I never ask for presents or drop hints about them. Once you accept a gift, after all, you can hardly refuse to return the favor—and returning birthday gifts is such a nuisance. It was only after a relationship in high school that I discovered, to my surprise, just how many anniversaries one “must” celebrate between New Year's Day and the end of December. In the back-and-forth, I learned to write greeting cards and choose gift boxes, making up for a crucial gap in my education. I even began taking these occasions seriously, until a birthday when she gave me a cushion. A few weeks later, I discovered that she had already made up her mind to leave when she gave it to me. The gift had seemed a token of affection, in the spirit of “I have nothing in Jiangnan to send you, save this sprig of spring.” Now it was an empty shell: form without substance. I hastily put the cushion away, and with it every lovely illusion I had held about birthday gifts.

Time is a one-way arrow. We define it through physical constants, then pick a few points on the calendar to commemorate. If the aliens visited Earth again during the Spring Festival and saw the gongs and drums sounding across China, they would surely wonder why everyone was celebrating their planet completing another circuit around the sun. Presumably they would never have studied folklore, and would not understand how vast and profound a festival's significance can be to a cultural community.

And yet, today is my birthday. Nineteen years ago today, I first opened my eyes to this world and set out on a journey from which there is no turning back. For me, too, a birthday ought to hold a significance both vast and profound. On my birthday a year ago, I wrote, rather bleakly:

We give the past meaning through remembering. I can clearly recall this evening a year ago, when my family and I gathered at the Western restaurant outside Yali. It used to be my favorite destination after a week of classes ended on Saturday. Before long, though, the restaurant closed, and my parents moved to Changsha to stay with me while I studied; there was no longer any need for an extra treat. “I wonder where you'll spend next year's birthday,” they said. At the time, I thought the answer mattered enormously. Looking back, perhaps it did not matter so much—certainly not enough to justify all my anxiety about an unknown outcome before the college entrance examination. Many things are like birthdays: they have no special meaning in themselves. Yet every good wish really can make someone who cycles alone each day between dormitory, library, and classroom feel that they are not alone. On this at least somewhat distinctive day, I wish you happiness. May every day bring you happiness.

A year later, looking back at those words, I can still clearly sense how depressed I was. My first semester at university was a headlong scramble. Everything I thought I knew about studying and getting along with people stopped working, and the enormous academic pressure left almost no room to breathe. A few days ago, chatting with a high-school classmate, I joked that my greatest achievement since starting university was making enough new friends to fill a WeChat contact group. Life may still fall short of what I want, but I am much better able to handle university than I was a year ago. That is undoubtedly thanks to the help of many, many people. To a large extent, it is also why I can now make a proper occasion of writing a birthday post for myself.

This is certainly no conventional birthday post, and I never meant it to be one. But there is one thing I must say in closing: a birthday spent entirely alone is meaningless. Only on this one day of the year do we receive the distinguished title of birthday person. So, just as I did a year ago: “I wish you happiness. May every day bring you happiness!”
